\section{Introduction}\label{sec-intro}

Assessing the distribution of an observed variable is an issue of keen
interest to many areas of statistical practice. Density estimates are
often part of exploratory data analysis (EDA) to help an analyst
understand how the observed data are distributed. In addition, many
statistical procedures rely of specific distributional assumptions about
the data: classic parametric tests such as a t-test rely on the
assumption that the data come from a normal population; Traditional
linear regression analysis assumes the model residuals follow a normal
distribution; data that follows a multimodal distribution may lead a
practitioner to perform mixture modeling.

When data are fully observed there are a host of methods for estimating
the probability density function; here we focus on kernel density
estimation.

Suppose we have \(B\) imputed data sets and we estimate \(f\) using a
standard kernel density estimator given by

\begin{equation}
\label{eqn:kde}
\hat{f_b}(x) = \frac1{nh_b}K\left(\frac{x - x_i}{h_b}\right) \ \ b = 1, 2 \dots, B, 
\end{equation} where \(K\) is an appropriately chosen kernel function
and \(h_b\) is a bandwidth select, see \citet{simonoff2012smoothing} for
more details. We propose to estimate \(f\) by averaging over the
individual \(B\) estimators, that is \begin{equation}
\label{eqn:kde_combo}
\hat{f}(x) = \frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B\hat{f}_b.
\end{equation} Clearly, \(\hat{f}(x)\) is a proper density in that it
integrates to one and is non-negative everywhere.
